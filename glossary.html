Reference  ·  Glossary

Defence autonomy,
in plain English.

Autonomy has accumulated a lot of jargon, and much of it obscures more than it explains. This is our working glossary — the terms we use across this site, defined the way we would explain them to someone in the room. No marketing, no acronym soup.

The Vocabulary

The terms
that matter.

Grouped by how they are actually used rather than alphabetically — starting with the ideas everything else depends on.

§ 01 / GLOSSARY

Autonomy

The ability of a machine to carry out a task without a person directing each step. Autonomy is a spectrum, not a switch: a system may plan its own route while still asking a human before doing anything consequential.

Autonomous system

A platform that can sense its surroundings, decide what to do and act on that decision. At Rewind Dynamics this covers unmanned aerial, precision-guided and tactical platforms.

UAV / UAS

Unmanned aerial vehicle, or unmanned aerial system. UAV usually means the aircraft itself; UAS means the whole system — aircraft, ground control, communications and operators.

ISR

Intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance. The work of gathering information about an area or situation so that commanders can make informed decisions.

Surveillance

Watching an area persistently over time to understand what is normal and notice what changes.

Reconnaissance

Going to look at a specific place to answer a specific question, usually once and quickly.

Force protection

Measures that keep personnel, equipment and sites safe — including early warning of an approaching threat.

Sense–decide–act

The core loop every autonomous system runs, many times a second: take in the world, choose an action, carry it out, and repeat. Making that loop fast, reliable and safe is the central engineering problem.

Perception

Turning raw camera and sensor data into a usable understanding of the environment — what is there, where it is, and whether it matters.

Sensor fusion

Combining several different sensors into a single picture that is more reliable than any one sensor could be alone.

Edge AI

Running artificial intelligence on the platform itself rather than in a remote data centre, so the system stays effective without a constant communications link.

GPS-denied navigation

Knowing your position and finding your way when satellite navigation is jammed, spoofed or simply unavailable — typically by using vision and onboard sensors instead.

GNSS

Global navigation satellite system: the general term for satellite positioning networks, of which GPS is one.

SLAM

Simultaneous localisation and mapping. Building a map of an unknown space while also working out where you are within it.

Swarm

A group of autonomous systems that coordinate as one team — sharing what they see, dividing the task, and remaining useful even if some are lost.

Mother–child architecture

A two-tier design in which a larger carrier platform transports and coordinates a group of much smaller platforms, which do the close-in work. One of our active research directions.

Nano-drone

A very small unmanned aircraft, typically light enough to be carried in quantity and cheap enough to be treated as expendable.

SWaP

Size, weight and power — the three constraints that dominate the design of any small autonomous system. Every capability added costs something in at least one of them.

Man-portable

Small and light enough to be carried and deployed by one person alongside their existing equipment.

Dismounted

Operating on foot, away from a vehicle. Dismounted teams have the tightest constraints on what they can carry.

Tactical edge

The forward, close-range environment where information is scarce, links are unreliable and decisions are made under pressure.

Degraded link

A communications connection that has become slow, intermittent or unusable. Well-designed autonomy continues behaving sensibly when this happens.

Human-in-the-loop

An arrangement where the system cannot take a significant action until a person approves it.

Human-on-the-loop

An arrangement where the system acts on its own but a person supervises continuously and can intervene or stop it at any time.

Human in command

Our own framing, and our operating principle: machines carry out tasks, but people set the rules, define the limits and remain responsible for the outcome.

Autonomy stack

The layered software architecture that produces autonomous behaviour — from low-level control, through perception and planning, up to mission and oversight.

Guidance, navigation and control

The three related jobs of deciding where to go, knowing where you are, and making the platform actually do it.

Simulation-first

Developing and proving behaviour in software before committing it to hardware, because software tests are faster, safer and repeatable.

After-action assessment

Reviewing a recorded run honestly to establish what went right, what went wrong and why — the discipline our name refers to.

Payload

Whatever a platform carries to do its job — usually sensors. Payload always competes with endurance for the same limited energy.

Endurance

How long a platform can operate before it must return, recharge or refuel.

Teleoperation

Direct remote control of a machine by a person. Distinct from autonomy, where the machine decides for itself within set limits.

Loitering

Holding a position or orbit over an area of interest, staying on station rather than transiting through — the behaviour that makes persistent surveillance possible within a platform's endurance.

Waypoint

A defined point on a planned route. A system flying waypoints follows a sequence of them autonomously; the interesting engineering is everything that happens when the world does not match the plan between them.

Endurance

How long a platform can operate before it must return, recharge or refuel. Endurance competes directly with payload and compute for the same limited energy, so every added capability costs time on station.

Payload

Whatever a platform carries to do its job — usually sensors such as electro-optical or infrared cameras. Payload always trades against endurance, because both draw from the same energy budget.

Gimbal

A stabilised mount that isolates a sensor from the platform's own motion, so the picture stays steady while the aircraft manoeuvres. Without it, onboard perception would be fighting the airframe's every movement.

Circular error probable (CEP)

The standard measure of delivery accuracy: the radius within which half of all deliveries fall. Every element of a guidance chain contributes error, and CEP expresses the total.

Terminal guidance

The final phase of guidance, where a seeker senses the actual scene — by laser, infrared image or radar — and refines aim onto the specific designated point rather than trusting coordinates alone.

Semi-active laser

A guidance method that homes on laser energy reflected from a point an operator or aircraft actively designates. Precise, and it keeps a human explicitly in the loop, because someone must designate the point.

Datalink

The radio link between an unmanned platform and its ground control station. In defence it is assumed to be intermittent, contested and sometimes absent, which is why the intelligence has to live on the platform.

Ground control station

The command position from which an operator sets a mission, watches the reported picture, and intervenes. In an autonomous system the operator talks to this, supervising rather than flying.

Lost-link routine

The pre-defined behaviour a platform follows when its datalink degrades or drops — continue, orbit, or return to a known point where contact can be re-established. Chosen in advance, never improvised.

Electro-optical / infrared (EO/IR)

A pairing of a visible-light camera and a thermal camera. EO gives imagery a human can confirm; IR works at night and in low light. Both need line of sight and are degraded by cloud, dust and smoke.

Low, slow, small (LSS)

The class of aerial target — flying low, moving slowly, physically small — that conventional air-defence sensing was never designed for, and that makes small drones so difficult to detect.

Vigilance decrement

The measured decline in human attention on a low-event monitoring task over time. It is why sustained surveillance is a poor fit for people and a good fit for autonomy.

Attrition

The loss of individual systems during operation. A design that degrades gracefully under attrition — losing capability in proportion rather than failing outright — is what makes cheap, numerous platforms viable.

These are working definitions written for clarity, not formal doctrine — usage varies between organisations and countries. If you think one of them is wrong or could be clearer, tell us and we'll fix it.
