System 03  ·  Tactical Miniature

Tactical miniature
systems.

Small, man-portable autonomous systems for close-range and dismounted use. When size, weight and power are tightly limited, simplicity and ruggedness matter more than anything — and that constraint is exactly what makes the problem interesting.

The Idea

Capability you can
carry.

§ 01 / OVERVIEW

The tactical edge is where most decisions actually get made — close, cluttered, and often without a reliable link to anyone else. A system that helps there has to be small enough to carry, quick enough to deploy, and self-sufficient enough to work without a support chain.

That is a brutal engineering constraint, and a clarifying one. Every gram and every watt has to justify itself. What survives the cut tends to be the capability that genuinely matters: see a bit further, understand a bit more, and do it without adding to what someone already has to carry.

Design Principles

Small, rugged, and honest about limits.

A tactical system earns its place by being dependable and unobtrusive. If it needs babysitting, it is a liability rather than an asset.

  • Compact autonomyMeaningful capability running on small, low-power hardware — the intelligence lives on the device.
  • Fast to deployUsable within moments by someone who has other things to worry about.
  • Light on logisticsMinimal support, minimal setup, minimal training overhead.
  • Works aloneContinues to be useful without a constant link back to anyone.

Why It's Hard

Everything is a
trade-off.

Miniature systems remove every comfortable assumption you get to make on a larger platform.

§ 02 / ENGINEERING
01

Compute in a matchbox

Running capable perception and autonomy on hardware with a fraction of the power a larger platform can spare.

02

Energy is everything

Endurance, compute and sensing all draw from the same small battery. Every choice costs another.

03

Ruggedness

Small things get dropped, knocked and rained on. Surviving ordinary handling is a hard requirement.

04

Simplicity under stress

The interface has to make sense to someone who is tired, busy and not thinking about your product.
