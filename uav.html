System 01  ·  Unmanned Aerial

Unmanned aerial
systems.

Autonomous aircraft that fly a mission and report what matters, with the operator supervising rather than piloting. We develop UAVs for intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR) and for force protection — systems that can watch, understand and report from positions we would rather not put people in.

The Idea

Fewer pilots.
More understanding.

§ 01 / OVERVIEW

A conventional drone moves the pilot from the cockpit to the ground. That is useful, but it still consumes a trained person for every aircraft in the air. Autonomy changes the ratio: the aircraft handles flying, and the person handles judgement.

Our emphasis is on the part that is genuinely hard — autonomy that holds up when the picture is incomplete. Reliable navigation, useful onboard perception, and sensible behaviour when conditions, weather or communications turn difficult. An aircraft that only works on a clear day with a strong link is not an aircraft you can plan around.

What It Does

Built around the mission, not the airframe.

The airframe is a means to an end. What determines whether a UAV is useful is the software: how it plans, what it notices, and how it behaves when something goes wrong.

  • Autonomous flight & navigationPlan, fly and adjust a route without a person on the sticks — including where satellite navigation is unreliable.
  • Onboard perceptionDetect and track what matters in real time, on the aircraft, and report it clearly rather than dumping raw video.
  • Resilient behaviourSensible, predictable action when the link degrades, the weather turns, or the picture is incomplete.
  • Operator supervisionA person sets the task and can intervene at any moment — command stays human.

Hard Problems

What we're actually
working on.

The engineering that decides whether an autonomous aircraft is a demo or a dependable tool.

§ 02 / ENGINEERING
01

Navigation without GPS

Satellite signals can be jammed, spoofed or simply absent. Knowing where you are using what you can see and sense is foundational, not optional.

02

Perception that earns trust

Detecting and tracking reliably in clutter, poor light and bad weather — and being honest about uncertainty rather than guessing confidently.

03

Degraded-link autonomy

Continuing the mission sensibly when the radio link thins or drops, then re-joining cleanly when it returns.

04

Endurance vs. payload

Every gram of sensor costs time in the air. Getting useful capability inside a strict weight and power budget is a constant trade.

05

Safe failure

What the aircraft does when something breaks matters more than what it does when everything works.

06

Clean hand-off

Handing control between autonomy and operator without confusion about who is flying.

Anatomy

What is actually
on board.

An unmanned aircraft is six subsystems that have to agree with each other several hundred times a second. Understanding what each one does is the fastest way to understand why autonomy is hard.

§ 03 / ANATOMY
01

Airframe & propulsion

The physical aircraft — fixed-wing for endurance and range, rotary or hybrid for hovering and confined launch. This choice sets the mission envelope before a line of software is written: you cannot give an airframe endurance it does not have.

02

Flight control system

The autopilot. It runs the inner control loop that keeps the aircraft stable — attitude, airspeed, altitude — hundreds of times a second, and executes the outer loop that follows a route. A person supervising a mission is talking to this, not flying the aircraft.

03

Inertial measurement unit

Accelerometers and gyroscopes sensing linear acceleration and rotation rate. Integrated over time they yield attitude and position without any outside reference — unjammable, but drifting steadily, which is why they are always paired with something that corrects them.

04

Navigation solution

An attitude and heading reference fusing the IMU with satellite navigation, barometric altitude, magnetometer and airspeed. This fused estimate — not any single sensor — is what the aircraft actually believes about where it is, and it carries a confidence value with it.

05

Payload & gimbal

The reason the aircraft exists. Typically a stabilised gimbal carrying electro-optical and infrared cameras. The gimbal isolates the sensor from airframe motion so the picture stays steady while the aircraft manoeuvres.

06

Datalink & ground control

The radio link to the ground control station, where an operator sets tasking, watches the picture and intervenes. The link is assumed to be intermittent, contested and sometimes absent — which is exactly why the intelligence lives on the aircraft.

A Mission, Start To Finish

What actually
happens.

Stripped of jargon, an autonomous sortie runs through the same sequence every time. Most of it involves no human input at all — which is the entire point.

  • Plan and uploadAn operator defines the objective — an area to cover, a route to follow, a point to observe — plus the limits the aircraft must not cross. This is the moment human command is exercised most concretely.
  • Launch and climbThe flight control system takes the aircraft off and climbs to its transit altitude on its own, holding attitude and airspeed against wind it has never seen before.
  • Transit and executeThe aircraft navigates to the area and executes the task, adjusting its route as wind, weather or the picture changes. Perception runs continuously on board, reporting what matters rather than streaming raw video.
  • Lost linkIf the datalink degrades or drops, the aircraft does not stop thinking. A defined lost-link routine takes over — continue, orbit, or return to a known point where contact can be re-established — chosen in advance, never improvised.
  • Recover and reviewThe aircraft returns and lands autonomously. Every second of the sortie is recorded, replayed and assessed honestly afterwards — the discipline our name refers to.
